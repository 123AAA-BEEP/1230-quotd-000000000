1. **Geographic scarcity is documented in cost content:** low-radon (EPA Zone 3) states have limited certified-contractor availability and higher prices, while Zone 1 states have competitive markets — https://www.homehazardsreport.com/blog/radon-mitigation-cost (snippet-derived). Scarce-market dynamic varies by geography — matches Quotd's scarce-market treatment concept.
2. **State programs actively steer consumers to find-a-contractor lists** (e.g. NC: https://www.ncdhhs.gov/divisions/health-service-regulation/north-carolina-radon-program/radon-mitigation; WI: https://www.dhs.wisconsin.gov/radon/reduce-radon.htm) — official acknowledgment that finding a qualified installer is the consumer's core problem; many states license/register radon pros.
3. **Transaction-coupled demand:** Minnesota Dept of Health notes home radon tests fell ~39% when fewer buyers got inspections (via https://www.health.state.mn.us/communities/environment/air/radon/radonre.html + real-estate radon resources https://sosradon.org/real-estate-testing, https://www.nar.realtor/residential-real-estate/home-inspections/radon-key-messages-and-resources) — demand rides real-estate volume.

## Volume hints

- No keyword-volume numbers found (searched). **Google volume unknown.**
- **Seasonality: winter-peaking.** Radon accumulates in sealed winter homes; testing (the demand trigger) peaks in heating season (https://ecosense.io/blogs/blog/why-do-radon-levels-fluctuate; IOP study https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/2752-5309/acdcb3). **January is EPA National Radon Action Month** (https://www.lung.org/clean-air/indoor-air/indoor-air-pollutants/radon/radon-action-month) — an institutional annual demand spike.
- Cost anchors: systems ~$800–$2,500; average ~$1,032 (HomeAdvisor/Angi/HomeGuide above). Testing $150–$700. Mid-low ticket, but need is health-driven and non-discretionary once a high test result lands.
- **Data-layer gold for the survival rule:** EPA radon zones by county, state licensing rules, and the 2024 PNAS high-resolution national radon map from massive indoor measurements (https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2408084121) — local radon-level data genuinely changes advice city by city.
